The Prisoner's Dilemma of NBA Superteams

Why NBA teams can rationally chase superstar-heavy rosters even when the arms race creates new costs, risks, and roster fragility.

By S. Brunello · September 2026
View the Dataset →

NBA teams don’t build their rosters in a vacuum. Every superstar signing, blockbuster trade, or all-in move changes the decisions facing all the teams in the league. Once one team decides to escalate, everyone else has to choose to either keep up or risk being left behind.

This is where the Prisoner’s Dilemma comes in.

I, like most other Economics students (I assume), was thrown this thought experiment early on in my academic career. It took me a few years of scrambling through courses to come back to it, but it turns out it’s a pretty useful way of thinking about how NBA teams respond to one another.

Before getting into that, though, it’s probably worth explaining what the Dilemma is.

The Prisoner’s Dilemma

The original dilemma has a few variations, but for the sake of argument we can keep it simple. Two members of a criminal gang are arrested and separated. The police do not have enough evidence to convict either of them on the main charge, so if both stay silent they will each serve one year on a lesser offence.

However, each prisoner is offered the chance to testify against the other. If one testifies while the other stays silent, the one who talks goes free while the other serves three years. If both testify, they each serve two.

Since neither prisoner can communicate with the other, each has to make the decision independently. If we assume both are rational and only care about minimizing their own sentence, testifying becomes the best response either way: if the other stays silent, you go free, and if they testify, you serve two years instead of three.

The problem is that when both follow that logic, they each end up serving two years, even though both staying silent would have left them better off.

Prisoner A Stays Silent A: 1 year
B: 1 year 		A: 3 years
B: 0 years
Prisoner A Testifies A: 0 years
B: 3 years 		A: 2 years
B: 2 years
A basic Prisoner’s Dilemma. Each prisoner has an individual incentive to testify even though mutual silence produces the better collective outcome.

How is this relevant to the NBA, you ask? It may be helpful here to look at a specific example.

Cleveland vs. Golden State

In the summer of 2014, LeBron is returning to the Cleveland Cavaliers after four years in Miami. Cleveland already has Kyrie Irving and quickly makes it clear that it is not interested in waiting around, trading No. 1 pick Andrew Wiggins, Anthony Bennett, and draft capital for Kevin Love.

Later in the season, they add J.R. Smith and Timofey Mozgov, rounding out a roster built to compete immediately.

At this stage, though, Cleveland is not reacting to Golden State. This is simply a classic “win-now” strategy: sacrifice cheap young talent and future assets for an established star while LeBron is still at his peak.

Golden State, meanwhile, is taking a very different route. The Warriors had made the playoffs in each of the previous two seasons, but they were still developing internally rather than assembling stars from elsewhere.

Mark Jackson had just been replaced by Steve Kerr, Draymond Green was becoming a full-time starter, and the now-iconic Curry-Klay-Green core was beginning to take shape. Of course, we know what that core eventually becomes, but let’s not get ahead of ourselves.

2014–15

Let’s return to the beginning of the 2014–15 season. To recap, on one side of the ring we have the star-studded Cavs, carrying massive expectations and widely projected to contend for the title.

In the other corner are the Warriors, viewed as a dangerous playoff team but still surrounded by uncertainty under first-year head coach Steve Kerr.

As the season progresses, though, Golden State quickly proves it belongs in a different category. The Warriors finish with a league-best 67 wins before meeting Cleveland in the Finals and taking the championship in six games, 4–2.

2015–16

For the 2015–16 season, Cleveland had to adapt to Golden State’s style of play. The Warriors’ biggest strengths were their three-point shooting and small, switchable lineups, which created constant spacing, mismatches, and fast-paced scoring opportunities.

Cleveland responded by building a roster that could better survive that kind of game. They added veteran wing Richard Jefferson and traded for Channing Frye, a 6’11” big who could stretch the floor and pull opposing centers away from the paint, opening driving lanes for LeBron and Kyrie.

These moves were not made solely with Golden State in mind, but it is hard to ignore how clearly they helped Cleveland match up with the Warriors’ style.

Meanwhile, Golden State simply doubled down on what had worked the year before, and it worked even better. The Warriors finished with a league-record 73–9 record, Curry became the first unanimous MVP, and Golden State entered the Finals as the favorite.

Then the unexpected happened.

LeBron and Kyrie exploded offensively, Tristan Thompson punished Golden State on the glass, and Cleveland completed the comeback to win the championship 4–3.

Summer 2016

Golden State still had an incredible lineup despite the loss in the Finals. They had Curry, Thompson, Green, and Iguodala, along with a coach who already knew exactly how to get the best out of them.

You would expect them to improve the bench, tweak the center rotation, and run it back.

Instead, they add Kevin Durant.

It is important to note here that a major reason Golden State was even able to make this move was the sudden jump in the salary cap to $94.143 million following the NBA’s new television deal.

The cap increased by roughly 34.5% in a single offseason, creating an unusual amount of space at exactly the right time for the Warriors.

Suddenly, Golden State could roll out some combination of Curry, Klay, Durant, Green, and Iguodala, creating a very serious problem for Cleveland.

The Cavs, already loaded with expensive stars, did not have the luxury of simply responding by adding another MVP of their own. Instead, they tried to increase their firepower around the margins, most notably by trading for Kyle Korver.

Essentially, Cleveland could not completely stop the Warriors’ offense, so they tried to build enough shooting of their own to keep up with it.

But it wasn’t enough.

Golden State took the 2017 Finals 4–1, with Durant winning Finals MVP, and the arms race had officially moved to another level.

At this point, Cleveland’s problem wasn’t simply how they could improve their roster. They had to keep pace with a rival that had moved the bar seemingly beyond reach.

Adding Korver made the Cavs better in absolute terms, but the NBA isn’t an absolute game. Teams are only as good as their competitors’ weaknesses, and the Warriors seemed to have none.

The payoff from investing in a stronger team depends on both how much better you become and how your competitors are improving at the same time.

Korver made Cleveland a better team, but that improvement meant far less once Golden State had added Durant.

This is where we begin to see the Prisoner’s Dilemma take shape, where both teams have an incentive to keep escalating because neither can be the one that stands still.

So What Does the Data Say?

Having seen the arms race play out in real time, we can finally bring this all back to economics.

The Cavs and Warriors are obviously only one example, but they give us a pretty clean way of thinking about how superteams can affect both their direct competitors and the league around them.

For four straight seasons, the same two teams met in the Finals. That alone should tell us that something unusual was happening at the very top of the league.

But before getting too far ahead of ourselves, let’s go back to basics.

For the purposes of this article, I am defining a superteam as a roster built around multiple established stars whose combined talent gives them a major competitive advantage over the rest of the league.

The logic behind building one is pretty straightforward. Teams want to win championships, and adding another superstar should, in theory, increase the chances of doing exactly that.

If winning the Championship is represented by C = 1

and not winning by C = 0,

E[C] = P(Championship)

That is really just a slightly more complicated way of saying that if a team wants to maximize the expected value of its season, it should try to maximize its probability of winning the title.

When a team commits cap space, draft assets, roster depth, or future flexibility to another star, this is what it is ultimately chasing: a larger P(Championship).

And there is an obvious reason why teams are willing to do this. Adding another superstar can raise a team’s ceiling far beyond what could realistically be achieved by tweaking the eighth man in the rotation.

But the trade-off, at least in theory, is that more and more of the team’s value becomes tied to a small number of players.

Just look at the 2021 Nets. Brooklyn had Durant, Harden, and Irving, a trio that on paper looked borderline unfair.

But when injuries took Harden and Irving out of the equation, Durant was suddenly left carrying a roster whose entire ceiling had been built around the presence of all three.

It’s fairly simple math really. If one player disappears from a balanced roster where the value is spread more evenly, the decline may be manageable.

If one superstar disappears from a team whose entire identity is built around three of them, a huge share of its offensive creation, shooting, or defensive value disappears too.

In other words, superteams are theoretically trading some robustness for a much higher ceiling.

And that trade-off matters most in the playoffs. Rotations get shorter, weaknesses get targeted more aggressively, and one missing star can matter far more than it did during a random Tuesday night game in January.

Of course, simply stating all of this as fact would be a bit lazy, so I wanted to see whether the numbers actually backed it up.

To do this, I looked at 11 separate team-seasons, made up of six superteam cases and five comparison teams.

I used four main measures: Core-3 Win Share concentration, average core Net On/Off, core availability, and value-weighted games missed.

The first result was exactly what you would expect.

The superteams in my sample entered their seasons with an average implied championship probability of roughly 35%, compared with about 7% for the comparison teams.

That is a pretty massive gap.

So whatever else we may say about the long-term risks of superteams, they seem to accomplish the thing they are actually built to do, as they raise expectations of winning the title.

Now, it is worth noting that these probabilities are not some objective truth handed down from the basketball gods.

They are betting-market expectations, so not guaranteed outcomes. Still, they give us a useful way of measuring how much championship value the market thought these rosters had before the season even began.

This is where I expected the rest of the data to follow the same story.

It did not.

Is the Value Really More Concentrated?

My original assumption was that superteams would have a much larger share of their value concentrated in their three best players.

That would make sense, right? If you build a roster around three superstars, surely those three players should account for far more of the team’s production than they would on a more balanced contender.

Instead, the core three accounted for roughly 48% of team wins through Win Shares among the superteams in my sample, compared with roughly 50% among the comparison teams.

On/off performance tells basically the same story. The average core-player Net On/Off was +7.1 for the superteams and +7.7 for the comparison group.

Figure 2 Core Value Concentration by Team-Season

So this was the first point where the data forced me to admit that my original idea was too simple.

Superteams were not uniquely concentrated around their three best players. In fact, some of the comparison teams were even more statistically concentrated.

Denver is probably the clearest example. Nikola Jokic can create an enormous amount of statistical concentration almost by himself, but that does not mean the Nuggets were constructed in the same way as the Durant Warriors or the Durant-Harden-Irving Nets.

This forced me to rethink what actually separates a superteam from an ordinary contender.

Simply having three statistically important players clearly is not enough. Teams like Denver can be heavily dependent on a small core without ever going through the same kind of superstar aggregation.

The difference seems to lie less in where the value eventually ends up and more in how aggressively the roster is constructed to put that value there.

Superteams are created through major salary commitments, trades, free-agent signings, and, quite often, the sacrifice of depth or future flexibility elsewhere.

So, with my original Core-3 idea looking a bit shaky, I shifted my focus towards what I was really trying to measure in the first place: fragility.

Does Building a Superteam Create Fragility?

Here, the difference becomes a little clearer.

Across the superteams in the sample, the three core players were available for roughly 81% of their eligible games, compared with around 85% for the comparison teams.

That is not some enormous gap, and I would not pretend that it is, but the difference becomes more interesting once we look at value-weighted games missed.

Superteams recorded an average value-weighted games-missed figure of 6.19, compared with 4.98 for the comparison teams.

The logic behind this measure is pretty simple because not every missed game is equal.

Losing your twelfth man for ten games is not the same thing as losing Kevin Durant for ten games.

A regular games-missed stat treats those two absences equally, when the actual effect on the team obviously is not.

Value-weighted games missed tries to account for this by giving greater weight to absences from more productive players.

Essentially, it asks a more useful question: how much actual team value disappears when these players are unavailable?

Back to Brooklyn

And once again, we arrive back at the 2021 Nets.

Brooklyn had the highest value-weighted absence figure of any team in my sample.

Durant, Harden, and Irving gave them an absurd ceiling when all three were available, but that ceiling was only useful if all three were actually on the court.

Their regular-season numbers already show how exposed the roster was to star availability, and the playoffs gave us an even clearer example of what that exposure could look like when injuries arrived at the worst possible time.

Once Harden and Irving were compromised, the theoretical value of having three stars did not disappear entirely, but a huge part of it became inaccessible.

Brooklyn still had Kevin Durant, which is a pretty good Plan B, but the superteam they had built was no longer really a superteam in practice.

Now, I should be clear about what these numbers do and do not tell us.

They do not show that superteams cause injuries. That would be ridiculous.

Injuries are random, the sample is small, and several of the comparison teams were also heavily dependent on a small number of stars.

The difference between 81% and 85% availability is also nowhere near large enough for me to declare that all superteams are inherently more fragile.

What the data does suggest is something narrower.

Superteams can raise the potential payoff enormously, but they also leave teams exposed when a large share of that top-end value becomes unavailable.

Back to the Prisoner’s Dilemma

This is where we should return to the Prisoner’s Dilemma.

A team may know that concentrating resources into a handful of stars comes with risks. It may know that its bench will be weaker, its flexibility will shrink, and that an injury to one star could suddenly leave it without much of a Plan B.

But then imagine being Cleveland in 2017, facing off against the Avengers assembled by the Warriors who take the form of Curry, Durant, Thompson, and Green.

You could choose the safer option and keep more depth, protect your future assets, and spread your value around the roster.

But you still have to beat Golden State. That’s the issue.

The safer roster may be better constructed in isolation, but the NBA does not exist in isolation.

A team is only trying to be better than the team standing in front of it.

If your rival has already pushed the talent threshold beyond what your current roster can handle, chasing another star can remain the rational response even if you know it creates new risks elsewhere.

That is the context in which the Prisoner’s Dilemma takes shape.

Each team has an incentive to escalate because neither wants to be the one that stands still.

And if everyone responds the same way, the league can enter an arms race where teams repeatedly sacrifice flexibility, depth, and future resources because the alternative looks even worse.

The NBA’s Solution

Of course, the NBA has changed quite a bit since the peak of Cavs-Warriors.

The 2023 CBA, and more specifically the introduction of the second salary-cap apron, has changed the economics behind this arms race quite significantly.

The league has not banned teams from collecting stars. That would probably be both incredibly difficult and a bit ridiculous.

If you draft three future Hall of Famers, the NBA is not going to make you give one back.

Instead, the league has changed the cost of continuing to escalate.

Teams that cross the second apron now face restrictions on several of the tools they would normally use to keep improving an already expensive roster. This includes limits on salary aggregation in trades, certain exceptions, and future draft-pick flexibility.

The second apron was not introduced as some explicit “no more superteams” rule, and I would not pretend that it was.

But the league has been fairly clear about its broader goal of limiting the competitive advantages available to the highest-spending teams and creating a more level playing field.

Adam Silver has also pointed to the NBA’s recent stretch of different champions as evidence that the broader push toward competitive balance has been working.

This is interesting because the second apron does not really attack the superteam itself, rather the incentive structure around it.

Teams can still chase stars and they can still decide that a third superstar is worth the cost. They can still escalate.

But now that escalation comes with much greater consequences for the depth and flexibility needed to support those stars once they arrive.

So, in a way, the NBA has changed the Prisoner’s Dilemma itself.

The incentive to match your rival’s star power still exists, but the payoff from choosing to escalate is no longer as straightforward as it once was.

I don’t think this means the NBA has ended the arms race, but it has made joining it a more costly decision.

Conclusion

As I come to the end of writing this, I realize I got a bit too wrapped up in the Cavs-Warriors story and left much of the analysis crowded toward the end.

Hopefully, though, the history helped make the underlying point clearer.

The original idea behind this analysis was that NBA superteams resemble a Prisoner’s Dilemma: once one contender begins accumulating stars, its rivals have an incentive to do the same, even if repeated escalation leaves everyone sacrificing depth, flexibility, and long-term stability.

The data supports part of that story.

Superteams in this sample entered seasons with dramatically higher championship expectations, showing why teams are willing to accept those costs in the first place.

Where my original assumption was too simple was in treating star concentration itself as the defining source of fragility.

The comparison teams were often just as dependent on their best players, and some were even more statistically concentrated.

The clearer risk appeared in availability.

When a superteam commits so much of its ceiling to a few elite players, injuries to those players can erase much of the advantage the roster was built to create.

So the problem is not that superteams are inherently worse, or even inherently more fragile, but that the competitive environment can make teams rationally accept greater costs and risks simply because standing still may be worse.

The NBA’s second apron can therefore be seen as an attempt to alter that payoff structure, making continued escalation more expensive and giving teams a stronger reason to preserve depth and flexibility rather than endlessly chase the next star.

A Few Important Caveats

This is where I will leave the analysis. I would, of course, be remiss if I did not acknowledge its shortcomings.

Adding more teams would provide a more robust picture, as a single extreme case, such as the 2021 Nets, can move the averages substantially.

I also use a subjective definition of a superteam, which any reasonable fan could disagree with, particularly in cases like the 2021–22 Lakers.

My sample is highly selective as well: I chose strong contenders such as Denver and Boston to provide a meaningful comparison group, but they are not representative of the NBA as a whole.

Some of my metrics are also noisy and heavily influenced by teammates, opponents, bench quality, injuries, and substitution patterns.

Much of the quantitative analysis comes from the regular season because it provides more observations, even though my broader argument is ultimately about championship risk.

Losing Kyrie Irving for three playoff games, for example, can matter far more than losing him for fifteen games in January.

Maybe one day I will return to this with a larger sample and a more rigorous model, but for now I am still a college student with classes, clubs, and a dissertation that probably deserves more of my attention.

Still, I will defend the analysis on one point: while it cannot establish that constructing a superteam causes greater roster fragility, it can show how star-heavy teams performed and how exposed they became when their most valuable players were unavailable.
